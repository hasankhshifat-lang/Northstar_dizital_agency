# Topic research report — "The Crest" (As Printed, episode 7)

Per docs/content/scriptwriting-rules.md §2.G–H. Topic 6 in `videos/as-printed/topics.md` ("The 1918 flu, October
1918: local papers downplaying the epidemic"), the first open topic. The previous episode was Charles Ponzi (money and
fraud), so this is not two disasters in a row. Angle sharpened from "papers downplayed the flu" to one city and one
paper: Philadelphia's Evening Public Ledger printing the crest again and again before the deadliest week.

1. **Topic:** 30 Sept 1918, two days after the Liberty Loan parade on Broad Street, the Evening Public Ledger's front
   page: "Grip decreasing; danger passing, doctors believe", the epidemic "seems to have reached its crest". 7 Oct:
   Krusen believes "the disease had reached its peak". 8 Oct: "City 'over top' in fight on grip". 9 Oct: "Grip on
   decline", "high tide". 12 Oct: "City death toll trebled by grip", the worst week ever recorded, "under control".
   16 Oct: "Less grip here … list 466 deaths today". The record: the U.S. Public Health Service's weekly table
   (Public Health Reports, 22 Nov 1918) shows Philadelphia's deadliest week ended 19 Oct (4,218 deaths).
2. **Why viewers care:** the 1918 flu is evergreen and widely searched; everyone remembers "the worst is over"
   announcements; the parade is a well-known story, but the paper's own run of "crest" headlines is not.
3. **Central question:** when did Philadelphia's flu really peak, and why did the paper keep calling it early?
4. **Strongest angle:** the same paper printing the crest four times in ten days, then printing the record week,
   and a government table that dates the real peak. Fair to the paper: cases really were falling; deaths lagged.
5. **Hook:** "Grip decreasing. Danger passing, doctors believe." → "reached its peak" → "high tide", three pages in a
   row before the title card.
6. **Titles:** "The 1918 Flu Peaked Four Times, On Paper" · "Philadelphia's Paper Kept Calling the Flu's Peak (1918)" ·
   "Danger Passing: How a 1918 Paper Called the Flu Crest Early"
7. **Audience interest:** high (1918 flu evergreen; parade story widely shared; comment question: which headline would
   have made you stop believing it).
8. **Evergreen / trending:** evergreen.
9. **Visuals:** 9 real Evening Public Ledger pages (headlines lit under the loupe), the Public Health Reports table
   (government record), 4 photographs (Broad Street stereograph; St. Louis Red Cross Motor Corps; Seattle streetcar
   masks; Washington Red Cross ambulance station, a demonstration). Non-graphic throughout.
10. **Sources:** Library of Congress (Chronicling America; Prints & Photographs), U.S. Public Health Service (CDC
    Stacks), Influenza Encyclopedia (Univ. of Michigan), Morens et al. 2008, Brundage & Shanks 2008; see FACTS.md.
11. **Score:** curiosity 17/20 · emotion 12/15 · suspense 12/15 · demand 14/15 · unexpected 8/10 · visual 8/10 ·
    discussion 8/10 · research 5/5 = **84/100** (strong).

Rotation: ep. 1 disaster (Titanic), 2 war/press, 3 famous person, 4 science scare, 5 war origin, 6 money/crime,
7 public health.

Variation from the last five episodes (compliance §1.3): no title card first (the last five all opened title → read →
read → table); the hook is a montage of three pages on three dates; the record is a government statistical table, not
a newspaper; a seven-part timeline structure; the verdict compares dates, not numbers; the "why" section argues
both sides (cases did fall; deaths lag; wartime bond drive printed beside the toll, without claiming it caused the
headlines).

Compliance notes: health history, educational; no medical advice; nothing contradicts health authorities; no graphic
images (no patients' faces in distress, no bodies; the ambulance photo is a labelled staged demonstration); the
parade is not claimed to have caused the deaths; Krusen's motives are not guessed.

Script check (§15): first sentence is the printed surprise; the first 30 s give the claim (three times), the conflict
(a government table "says something very different") and the question, without revealing the date; parts end on
loops ("nine days later … a parade", "three days later the same paper printed this", "a different number was coming
in every day", "which week was the worst?"); the turn is the 12 Oct death toll (Part 5, ~55% in) and the record is the
PHS table (Part 7); the verdict is the compare shot; the ending answers both opening questions (peak week ending 19 Oct;
cases vs deaths, plus the wartime context); one comment question and one short like/subscribe line at the end; every
quote is in FACTS.md. Storyboard = `episode.json` / `mk.py` shot list (purpose comments in mk.py).
