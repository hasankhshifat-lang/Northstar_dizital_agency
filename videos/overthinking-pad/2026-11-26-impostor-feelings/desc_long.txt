What's in it
• Clance & Imes (1978) named the "impostor phenomenon" after working with more than 150 high-achieving women. It is not a psychiatric diagnosis.
• A 2020 systematic review (62 studies, 14,161 people) found impostor feelings in men and women, from teens to late career, with rates from 9% to 82% depending on how they were measured. No published study had tested a treatment.
• The "70%" figure is usually traced to a 2007 newspaper article; we couldn't find the original study, so treat it as a rough guess.
• The impostor cycle: over-preparing or putting things off, then crediting success to effort or luck instead of ability.
• Tewfik (2022): people with more impostor thoughts were rated as better with others (patients rated such doctors as better listeners), but the thoughts also lowered self-esteem. Not a reason to want them.
