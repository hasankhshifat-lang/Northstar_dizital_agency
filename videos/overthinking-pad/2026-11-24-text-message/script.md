**[0:38]** So why does your brain turn a two letter text into a verdict? How often is that verdict right? And what should you do the next time okay, with a period, lands on your screen?

### 1. THE MISSING VOICE  (0:51)

**[0:54]** In two thousand five, a team led by the psychologists Justin Kruger and Nicholas Epley ran a set of experiments on exactly this. In one of them, sixty students at Cornell came in, in pairs.

**[1:08]** Each person got a list of short sentences about campus life. Some were meant sarcastically. Some were meant seriously. Their job was to get the real meaning across to their partner.

**[1:19]** Here's the twist. Half of them typed their sentences into an email. The other half read them out loud into a tape recorder. Same sentences. Different channel.

**[1:29]** Before the partners saw anything, the senders predicted how many their partner would get right. And the email senders were just as confident as the people using their voice.

**[1:40]** The listeners got close to three out of four right. The readers? Their accuracy was no better than a coin flip.

**[1:48]** And the readers had no idea. They felt about as sure of their answers as the listeners did. Nearly nine out of ten, they believed... with the accuracy of a coin flip.

**[1:59]** So the problem isn't that text is hard to read. The problem is that it doesn't feel hard. Your brain hands you an interpretation... and stamps it, certain.

**[2:10]** Even in their smallest study, where most messages did get through, senders expected ninety seven percent of them to land. Eighty four percent did. Confident... and off.

**[2:23]** But surely this is about strangers, right? Your best friend's texts, you can read those. The researchers wondered the same thing.

**[2:32]** So they ran a bigger version at the University of Illinois. One hundred and fifty four pairs. Some were strangers. Some were friends. And this time, the messages carried sarcasm, seriousness, anger, or sadness.

**[2:49]** Over email, the readers believed they had understood almost nine in ten messages. They had actually got about six in ten. And here's the part that stings... friends were no more accurate than strangers.

**[3:03]** Knowing someone for years didn't fix it. Which raises a question. If the tone isn't in the text... where is your brain getting it from?

### 2. YOUR BRAIN FILLS THE GAP  (3:13)

**[3:15]** Think about what a voice carries. Pitch. Pauses. A little laugh at the end. A face adds a raised eyebrow, a smile. Text strips almost all of that away.

**[3:29]** But your brain doesn't like a gap. So it fills it. And the voice it uses to fill it... is yours.

**[3:36]** Kruger and Epley saw this from the sender's side. When you type a sarcastic line, you hear the sarcasm in your head as you write it. So it seems obvious. To you.

**[3:46]** In one study, they asked senders to read their own messages out loud in the opposite tone first. Sarcastic lines read seriously. Serious lines read sarcastically.

**[3:59]** And their overconfidence disappeared. Once they heard the message the way a stranger might hear it, their predictions finally matched reality.

**[4:08]** Now flip that around. When a text lands on your phone, you're the one supplying the voice. And the voice you supply depends on your mood, your history with that person... and what you're already afraid of.

**[4:20]** If you've been worrying that a friend is upset with you, the voice in your head reads okay... as cold.

**[4:28]** There's a reason that voice so often sounds negative. In a two thousand eight review of how emotion travels by email, the management researcher Kristin Byron argued that readers tend to see messages as more negative, or more neutral, than the sender meant.

**[4:44]** That was a theory paper, built on earlier studies, not one big experiment. But small pieces of evidence point the same way. Take that period.

**[4:55]** In a study at Binghamton University, a hundred and twenty six students read short exchanges where the reply was one word, like okay, or yup. When the reply was a text message, the version ending in a period was rated as less sincere.

**[5:12]** On a handwritten note, the period made no difference. The authors called it a modest study, with one age group. But it shows how much meaning we pour into a single dot.

**[5:24]** So your brain fills the gap. And it tends to fill it with something worse than what was sent. Which leads to the most useful question in this whole video. When your brain guesses wrong... which way does it usually lean?

### 3. THEY LIKE YOU MORE THAN YOU THINK  (5:39)

**[5:41]** Here's where it turns. If our errors were random, we'd guess too warm about as often as too cold. But in one area, the errors have a direction.

**[5:51]** In a two thousand eighteen study, the psychologists Erica Boothby, Gus Cooney, Gillian Sandstrom and Margaret Clark paired strangers for a conversation. Afterwards, each person rated how much they liked their partner... and guessed how much their partner liked them.

**[6:07]** People consistently underestimated it. Their partners liked them more, and enjoyed their company more, than they thought. The researchers called it the liking gap.

**[6:18]** And it wasn't just one awkward lab chat. They found it in first year students getting to know their dorm mates, and the gap lasted for months. They found it in adults at a personal development workshop too.

**[6:32]** Why? The researchers' best guess is that during a conversation we're busy judging our own performance, so we miss the signs that the other person is actually enjoying it.

**[6:42]** Now think about what that means for the okay on your screen. You're reading a message from someone who, on average, probably thinks better of you than you assume.

**[6:52]** And there's a second gap, about reaching out. In a series of experiments published in two thousand twenty two, Peggy Liu and her colleagues had people send a short note or a small gift to someone in their social circle.

**[7:05]** The senders guessed how much it would be appreciated. Then the people who got it said how they actually felt. Over and over, the senders underestimated it.

**[7:16]** And the gap was biggest when the message was a surprise... like a text out of the blue to someone you haven't talked to in a while. The exact text people keep not sending.

**[7:27]** Put it together. Text hides tone. Your brain fills in the missing voice with your fears. And on the question of what people think of you, those fears tend to run darker than the truth.

**[7:40]** One honest caveat. These are averages. Sometimes okay with a period really does mean someone's upset. The point isn't that your worry is always wrong. It's that your confidence in it isn't evidence.

### 4. WHAT ACTUALLY HELPS  (7:53)

**[7:55]** So what do you do when a short text sends your brain spinning? Here are three things, each one tied to the research.

**[8:03]** One. Read it again, in a different voice. Remember the senders who read their messages in the opposite tone, and suddenly saw how a stranger might hear them?

**[8:13]** Do the reader's version. Read okay in the warmest voice you can imagine. Then in a rushed voice, someone typing at a red light. If all of them fit the words, the words alone don't tell you which one is true.

**[8:27]** That's not pretending everything is fine. It's noticing that the cold version was one reading out of several... and that you picked it.

**[8:35]** Two. If it matters, ask. A quick, light question, like, all good? clears up in ten seconds what your brain could spin on for hours. In the Illinois study, even friends misread each other. Asking isn't needy. It's just accurate.

**[8:54]** Three. When it really matters, use your voice. In two thousand twenty one, Amit Kumar and Nicholas Epley asked two hundred people to reconnect with an old friend, by email or by phone.

**[9:08]** People expected a phone call to feel more awkward, so they leaned toward email. But the people who were assigned to phone felt a stronger bond. And the phone calls didn't feel any more awkward.

**[9:20]** In another study, strangers chatted by text, by audio, or by video. Hearing each other's voice made them feel more connected. Adding video didn't add much more.

**[9:32]** So the voice is the missing piece. Put it back, and most of the guessing goes away.

**[9:39]** And a bonus for when you're the sender. If a message could be read two ways, add the tone yourself. A few warm words, a ha, or an emoji does the job your voice would have done.

**[9:53]** None of this is therapy. If messages regularly leave you anxious for days, that's worth talking through with someone. But for the everyday okay, these three habits go a long way.

**[10:05]** So, back to that okay, with a period. Your brain heard it in a cold voice... because it had to pick a voice, and it picked the one you were afraid of.

**[10:15]** The research says that voice is often wrong, and wrong in a predictable direction. The person who sent it probably likes you more than you think... and was probably just busy.

**[10:27]** So tell me in the comments. What's the shortest text that ever sent your brain into a spiral? And if you like your psychology with the receipts, subscribe. There's a new page every week.
