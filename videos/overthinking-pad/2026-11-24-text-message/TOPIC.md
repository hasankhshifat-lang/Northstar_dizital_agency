Research: Kruger, Epley, Parker & Ng 2005 checked against the full paper (numbers below are from its text, not from press
summaries); the other papers checked on PubMed abstracts and university/APA releases (see FACTS.md). vidIQ was not used:

from public reach: the Kruger e-mail study is one of the most-covered findings in communication psychology (NPR, APA
Monitor, BPS), and "why do I overthink texts" / "ok with a period" are well-known everyday searches and memes.

1. **Proposed topic.** Why your brain reads short texts as cold or angry: tone is missing in text, the reader fills
   the gap with their own voice and fears, and on "what do they think of me" those fears run predictably darker.
2. **Why viewers care.** Nearly everyone has re-read a two-letter reply ten times. Concrete, daily, low-stakes but
   emotionally sharp, and shareable ("send this to your friend who texts 'ok.'").
3. **Central question.** When your brain turns "ok." into a verdict, how often is it right, and which way does it err?
4. **Strongest angle.** It isn't the message, it's the reader: a coin-flip accuracy that *feels* like 9-in-10 certainty,
   friends no better than strangers, and errors that lean negative (liking gap, reach-out gap).
5. **Opening hook.** "Your phone buzzes... And it says... okay. Just okay. With a period." (scene-first cold open,
   EXP-002 control).
6. **Three titles.**
   - Why Your Brain Reads Short Texts as Angry (chosen: plain promise, search-friendly)
   - "Ok." With a Period: Why One Text Ruins Your Whole Day
   - You're Misreading Their Texts (Psychology Proves It)
7. **Expected interest.** High and evergreen: texting anxiety is a large, young, US-heavy audience; most existing videos
   are opinion or etiquette, few use the actual experiments.
8. **Classification.** Evergreen.
9. **Visual opportunities.** The phone with "ok." as the recurring object; voice wave vs flat line; email vs tape
   recorder split; the coin flip; the CERTAIN stamp; the opposite-tone swap; the period as a giant dot; the liking-gap
   bars; the unsent "hey! been a while"; the voice-shaped puzzle piece; whip back to the couch at the end.
10. **Reliable sources.** Kruger et al. 2005 (JPSP); Byron 2008 (AMR, theory/review, labelled as such); Gunraj et al.
    2016 (Computers in Human Behavior, "modest study", labelled); Boothby et al. 2018 (Psych Science); Liu et al. 2022/2023
    (JPSP); Kumar & Epley 2021 (JEP: General). See FACTS.md.
