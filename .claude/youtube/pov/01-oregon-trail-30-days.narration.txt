What kills you first on the Oregon Trail? Not snakes, not bandits, but something you'll drink before breakfast.

It's spring, 1850. You're in Independence, Missouri, and you're about to walk two thousand miles to Oregon. You're not a real person - you're built from the diaries, guidebooks and records of the people who actually did this. Everything that happens to you happened to them.

You get thirty days. Let's see if you make it.

First, you shop. A guidebook from this year has a list for a family of three. Four yoke of oxen - that's eight animals - at fifty dollars a yoke. Two hundred dollars. A wagon and its canvas cover, another hundred.

Then food. The rule people repeat in letters home is two hundred pounds of flour per person. For three of you, that's six hundred pounds of flour before you've bought a single slab of bacon.

Here's the first surprise. You don't ride in the wagon. It's full. You walk beside it. Every mile.

You leave in spring for a reason. Leave too early and there's no grass for the oxen. Leave too late and the snow catches you in the mountains.

So the whole trail runs on one deadline. There's a rock in what will become Wyoming - Independence Rock. If you reach it by the Fourth of July, you're on schedule. If you don't, you're racing the winter.

Most days you'll make ten to fifteen miles. On foot. At the pace of an ox.

Then the rivers start. Some have ferries. Some you just have to cross.

Out here, accidents are the second biggest killer. Wheels roll over people. Guns go off by mistake. And rivers drown people - in front of their families, a few yards from the bank.

You get across. Plenty of people don't. And this still isn't the thing that kills you first.

By day twelve, every day is the same day. Up before light. Yoke the oxen. Walk. Stop at noon. Walk. Circle the wagons. Cook. Sleep on the ground.

Your boots are already going. Your feet are worse. And the wagon in front of you has started leaving things behind - a dresser, a stove - because the oxen can't pull it all.

You'll see a lot of that. People's whole lives, left in the grass.

Day nineteen. The man in the next wagon says he feels a little feverish. He's fine. He's eating.

By noon, he can't stand up.

By evening, you're helping dig his grave.

That's cholera. The Park Service says victims often died within twelve hours of the first symptoms. Healthy at breakfast. Gone by supper.

Here's the thing nobody on this trail knows yet. Cholera lives in dirty water. And thousands of people and animals are using the same rivers, the same campsites, the same water holes, one after another, all summer.

That cup you drank from on day one? That's your answer. Not snakes. Not bandits. The water.

Disease killed more people on this trail than anything else - cholera most of all. Historians argue about the total. The National Park Service estimates that nearly one in ten people who set out never made it.

The Park Service's own math puts it at ten to fifteen deaths for every mile of trail.

Day thirty. You're alive. You've walked maybe three or four hundred miles.

That means you have more than fifteen hundred to go. Independence Rock is still weeks ahead. The mountains are after that. And you'll be drinking from that river every single day.
