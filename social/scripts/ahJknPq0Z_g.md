It's 1849 in Richmond, Virginia. Henry Brown is enslaved. His wife and children have been sold away from him.
He decides to escape in the most impossible way. A free friend and a shoemaker help him build a wooden box:
3 ft long, 2 ft wide, just big enough to fold himself inside. He packs a little water and a few biscuits.
Then they nail the lid shut.
The box is addressed to an anti-slavery office in Philadelphia. It is loaded on a wagon, then a train, then a
steamboat, then another train. Workers toss it around. At one point, it is turned upside down. Henry hangs by
his head for hours, his eyes swelling, his heart pounding. He cannot make a sound. If anyone hears him, it is over.
27 hours later, the box reaches Philadelphia. The abolitionists pry it open, not knowing if he is alive.
Silence. Then a hand rises. Henry Brown sits up and says, "How do you do, gentlemen?" Then he sings a hymn of
thanks. He walked out of that box a free man. Would you have climbed inside?
