## Script (Jesse, eleven_v4)
1. [tense] At three forty-five in the morning, the Mississippi River started flowing backward. So what can push a whole river the wrong way?
2. February seventh, eighteen twelve. New Madrid, a frontier town on the Mississippi. For two months, the ground here has not stopped shaking. Huge earthquakes. Hundreds of aftershocks.
3. [tense] Then the biggest one hits. Under the river, the ground lifts up. The current turns around. Waves throw boats onto the banks. Waterfalls appear in the Mississippi.
4. New Madrid is destroyed. In Tennessee, the land sinks and fills with water. It becomes Reelfoot Lake... and it's still there today.
5. The first quake even woke people in Washington, D.C. And the fault under New Madrid is still active. [quietly] So remember... at three forty-five in the morning, the Mississippi River... (loops to line 1)

Fact check (only what the sources agree on): Feb 7 1812 shock ~3:45 a.m. local near New Madrid, M~7.5 (USGS, Wikipedia);
earlier main shocks Dec 16 1811 and Jan 23 1812 plus 200+ aftershocks (USGS); the river was seen running backward
(USGS NMSZ page: uplift "temporarily forced the Mississippi River to flow backwards"; USGS summary explains it as waves
and uplift of the riverbed; Eliza Bryan: current retrograde "for a few minutes"); temporary waterfalls from uplift
(Wikipedia, earthquaketracker.org); boats washed ashore by waves (Wikipedia); New Madrid destroyed (USGS, Wikipedia);
Reelfoot Lake formed in Tennessee by subsidence (USGS, Wikipedia); the first shock woke people in Washington, D.C. (USGS);
the zone is still active (USGS). Left out: Boston church bells (not in USGS/Wikipedia), steamboat New Orleans (not needed),
exact duration of the reversal (sources differ: "a few minutes" vs "hours" in later retellings).
