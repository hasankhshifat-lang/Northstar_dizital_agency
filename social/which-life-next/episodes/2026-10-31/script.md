# POV: You Survive the Locust Plague of 1874

## 01 Cold open
[tense] It's late July, 1874, and it's the best corn you've ever grown. Waist high, green, and it's going to carry your family through the winter. Then, in the afternoon, you see a cloud in the west. It isn't rain. It glitters in the sun. [pause] By the next morning, the corn is gone. Not damaged. Gone. [calm] Your name is Kit. This is your second summer on a homestead in Nebraska, and you're about to live through the biggest swarm of insects ever recorded. Would you have made it?

## 02 Everything rides on the corn
Here's the deal you signed. A hundred and sixty acres, free, if you live on it and farm it for five years. You've built a sod house. You've broken the prairie with a plow and a pair of oxen. And this year, everything rides on one field. The corn is your food. It's next year's seed. It's the only thing you can sell for cash. There is no insurance, no savings, and the nearest neighbor is a long walk away. If the corn fails, you have nothing between your family and the winter.

## 03 The cloud
[tense] The first thing you notice is the sound. A dry, rattling hiss, like a hard rain that never hits the ground. Then the light changes. The sun goes dim, the way it does before a storm, except the sky is clear. [pause] They come down all at once. Grasshoppers. Millions, and then more than anyone can count. They land on the fence, the roof, your arms, your face. They cover the ground so thick you can't take a step without crushing them. Farmers in Kansas said they arrived around the twenty-fifth of July. In Nebraska, the worst of it came in the same week. [calm] Scientists would later call them Rocky Mountain locusts. Out here, everyone just called them hoppers.

## 04 What they eat
Then they start eating. [tense] The corn first. They strip the leaves, then the stalks, then the ears. The garden goes next. Onions, turnips, potatoes, gone down into the ground. People threw blankets and sheets over their vegetables to save them, and the hoppers ate right through the cloth. [pause] In one Kansas county, a local history says they destroyed every stalk of corn and all the green growing things in two or three weeks. [calm] When they finally lift off and move on, the fields look like it's already winter. Brown, bare, and silent.

## 05 Fighting back
You try to fight. Everyone does. You beat them with branches. You light smoky fires along the edge of the field. You dig a ditch and sweep them into it. It barely matters. For every one you kill, a thousand more are still eating. In 1877, the federal government set up a commission to study the locusts, led by an entomologist named Charles Valentine Riley. His report described ways to fight back, like ditches, fire, and a sheet-iron pan smeared with coal tar that farmers dragged across the fields to catch the hoppers. Farmers called it a hopperdozer. But in the summer of 1874, all you had was your hands.

## 06 Stay or leave
Now you have to decide. [tense] Winter is coming, and there's no food in the field and no money in the house. If you leave the claim, you lose the five years. If you stay, you might starve. [pause] Many families gave up and went back east. [calm] But something unusual happened. The federal government allowed homesteaders hit by the grasshoppers to leave their land for a while, to go find work somewhere else, without losing their claim. So you make the hardest choice on the frontier. You leave your family in the sod house and walk east to find wages, anywhere you can get them.

## 07 The winter of hunger
That winter, the whole country hears about the grasshopper sufferers. In Kansas, the governor calls a special session of the legislature in September. In Nebraska, the state approves fifty thousand dollars in bonds to buy seed for next spring. The Army starts handing out surplus clothing and supplies, and Generals John Pope and Edward Ord send reports to Washington about families with nothing left. [tense] In February 1875, President Ulysses S. Grant sends a message to Congress about the sufferers in Kansas and Nebraska and asks it to approve the relief. [calm] Historians count it as one of the earliest times the federal government stepped in to help ordinary people after a natural disaster. Help came from private people too. Churches and aid societies in the East collected food, clothing and seed, and the railroads carried the donations west for free. Kansas issued seventy-three thousand dollars in state relief bonds. [tense] But the aid had rules. To get it, a family had to show they were destitute, with nothing left to sell. [pause] Picture standing in line to prove you've lost everything. [calm] Your family gets through the winter on that help. Barely.

## 08 The biggest swarm ever recorded
In the spring, you plant again with donated seed, and you watch the ground. Because the hoppers didn't just eat and leave. They laid eggs, and in some places the young ones hatched right out of the soil you'd just planted. And the plague wasn't done. It would keep coming back in waves through the late 1870s, and by 1877 the Kansas legislature was passing laws to help farmers destroy grasshoppers. [tense] But the biggest wave of all came in 1875. In Plattsmouth, Nebraska, a man named Albert Child watches a swarm pass overhead. He times how long it takes, he checks its speed, and he uses telegraph reports from other towns to measure how far it stretches. [pause] His estimate: about eighteen hundred miles long and a hundred and ten miles wide. Roughly a hundred and ninety-eight thousand square miles. That's bigger than the entire state of California. [calm] Nobody knows exactly how many locusts that was. Estimates run from about three and a half trillion to twelve and a half trillion. Guinness World Records has listed the high estimate as the greatest concentration of animals ever recorded. People named it Albert's Swarm. [tense] Imagine being under it. The sun going dim for hours, the hiss in the air, and knowing that wherever it lands, there won't be a green leaf left.

## 09 Then they vanished
Here's the part almost nobody knows. [tense] The Rocky Mountain locust doesn't exist anymore. [pause] After the 1870s, the swarms got smaller. Then they stopped. The last living Rocky Mountain locusts were collected in 1902, in Manitoba, Canada. After that, nothing. An insect that once covered an area bigger than California, gone in less than thirty years. For decades, scientists had no idea why.

## 10 Who killed them
The best answer came from an entomologist named Jeffrey Lockwood at the University of Wyoming. The locusts didn't lay their eggs everywhere. They laid them in a few river valleys in the Rocky Mountains, where they waited out the bad years. And those river valleys were exactly where settlers were moving in, plowing, flooding fields, and grazing cattle. [pause] To prove it, Lockwood went looking for the bodies. In the mountains of Montana and Wyoming there are glaciers where swarms of locusts were caught by storms and frozen into the ice. He climbed up to them and dug out locusts that had been preserved for more than a century. [pause] What he found pointed back to those valleys. Without knowing it, farmers had wiped out the nurseries. [calm] The people the locusts nearly starved were the people who finished them off.

## 11 Verdict
Kit's verdict: you made it. You lost a whole harvest, you spent a winter on charity, and you left your family in a sod house to find work. You watched the sky every summer after that, waiting for the glitter in the west. [pause] But you kept the claim. And the insect that nearly broke you is gone for good, frozen in a few mountain glaciers. Think about that the next time you see a grasshopper in a field.
