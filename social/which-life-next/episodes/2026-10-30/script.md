# POV: Your First Winter as a Homesteader (1888)

Narration text for ElevenLabs (tags in brackets are stripped for captions).

## 01 Cold open
[tense] It's January twelfth, 1888, and it's the warmest morning you've had in weeks. Close to fifty degrees. Your kids go off to school without their heavy coats. Why would they need them? [pause] By tonight, it will be twenty-seven below zero. And before the sun goes down, the distance between living and dying on your farm will be about twelve feet. [calm] Your name is Kit. You came out here with almost nothing, for free land. This is your first winter on the Great Plains. Would you have made it?

## 02 The deal
Rewind to the spring of 1887. You're standing in a land office in Dakota Territory, and the deal on the table sounds too good to be true. Under the Homestead Act, the government will give you a hundred and sixty acres. The paperwork costs ten dollars, plus a two-dollar commission for the land agent. At the end, another six dollars. Eighteen dollars, total, for a farm. There's only one catch. You have to live on that land, build a home on it, and farm it, for five straight years. Then two neighbors have to swear you did it. Only then is the land yours. They call it proving up. You sign. Thousands of people are signing. Nebraska, just to the south, went from about a hundred and twenty-three thousand people in 1870 to over a million by 1890. Everybody wants their hundred and sixty acres.

## 03 The house made of dirt
You get off the wagon, and the first thing you notice is what's missing. There are no trees. None. No logs for a cabin, no firewood, nothing to break the wind. So you build with the only thing out here there's plenty of. The ground. You cut the prairie into strips of sod, thick with grass roots, and stack them like bricks. Two rooms, if you're lucky. The walls are so thick they hold the summer heat out and the winter heat in. It's dark, it's dusty, and when it rains, the roof drips mud on the table. But it's yours. Almost.

## 04 Summer is for winter
Here's the thing nobody tells you before you sign. Out here, summer is when you get ready for winter. Because there's no wood, and coal costs more money than you have, you have to figure out what you're going to burn. Your kids walk the prairie gathering buffalo chips. Dried dung. It burns, and it's free. And you cut the tall, coarse grass that grows in the wet low spots, the sloughs, and you stack it, a whole summer's worth. [tense] Because when the snow comes, that stack is the only thing standing between your family and the cold. [calm] One homesteader's daughter remembered it decades later. On stormy days, her father and brothers took turns sitting in a little sod shed, twisting that slough grass into tight bundles, packing it into iron drums, and feeding it to the stove. One drum burned while they filled the next. All day.

## 05 Everything else that wants you gone
And winter is only one of the problems. Your nearest neighbor might be three miles away. Your nearest neighbor might be ten. One homesteading family in Dakota listed what they lived through: dry years, grasshoppers, hail storms, and range fires. [tense] A dry year means no crop. A grasshopper year means you watch your crop disappear in front of you. A prairie fire means you watch the horizon turn orange and you plow a firebreak as fast as your oxen can walk. [calm] And through all of it, the five-year clock keeps running. Leave, and you lose everything you put in.

## 06 The first storm
Your first real blizzard comes in December. The wind picks up, the snow goes sideways, and within minutes you can't see your own barn. But the cows still need milking and the oxen still need feeding. So you do what one Dakota father did, a story his daughter told her whole life. [tense] You tie one end of the clothesline to the corner of the sod house. You walk to the barn holding the other end. [pause] And you tie it there. Now there's a rope between your door and your animals, and as long as you don't let go of it, you can find your way back. [calm] It feels a little silly the first time. It won't feel silly in January.

## 07 The warm morning
Which brings us back to January twelfth, 1888. In Huron, Dakota Territory, the morning is in the forties. People are relieved. They leave the heavy coats at home. Your kids walk to the sod schoolhouse in the light jackets they wear in spring. [tense] Then, around midday, an arctic cold front sweeps across the plains. By noon in Huron it's twenty degrees. By the time the children are let out of school in the afternoon, it's ten below. [pause] And then it gets worse. The wind hits fifty miles an hour. The snow isn't deep, four to twelve inches at most stations, but it's so cold and so fine that it fills your eyes and your mouth and your lungs. [quietly] Tonight, Huron will reach twenty-seven below zero.

## 08 The schoolhouses
Every teacher on the prairie has the same terrible choice to make, at the same moment. Keep the children inside a sod schoolhouse with a small fire and no food, or try to get them home. [calm] Near Ord, Nebraska, the wind tore the door and the roof right off Minnie Freeman's schoolhouse. She was nineteen years old. She tied twelve of her students together with twine, picked up the youngest one in her arms, and led them through the storm to a farmhouse about half a mile away. Every one of them survived. [tense] In Plainview, Nebraska, another young teacher, Loie Royce, tried to walk three of her students to her home. It was less than ninety yards from the schoolhouse. [pause] They got lost. [quietly] When searchers found them the next morning, the children were gone.

## 09 Twelve feet
Now it's your turn. It's late afternoon, the storm is screaming, and you have animals in the barn that haven't been fed. You know the barn is right there. You've walked it a thousand times. [tense] Near Brookings, Dakota Territory, a seventy-year-old farmer named Ole Olesen Tisland went out to tend his livestock that day. [pause] [quietly] He was found twelve feet from his own house. [calm] Twelve feet. That's how blind this storm is. So you open the door, and you put your hand on the rope you tied in December. And you don't let go of it. Not once. Not to adjust your scarf, not to wipe your eyes. Out to the barn. Feed. And back along the rope. Your kids made it home before the worst hit, or they stayed in the schoolhouse with a teacher who wouldn't let them leave. Tonight, you're one of the lucky ones.

## 10 The count
When it finally blows itself out, rescue parties go out over towering drifts that have buried roads and homes. Some of the people lost in the storm aren't found for months. Nobody agrees on exactly how many died. The number most often cited is about two hundred and thirty-five. Some historians think it was as many as five hundred, because not every death on a remote farm was ever reported. [calm] And families everywhere asked the same question. Why did nobody warn us? The U.S. Army's Signal Service was in charge of weather forecasts, and the anger after this storm pushed the government to build a better forecasting system. Two months later, another surprise blizzard hit the Northeast and killed around four hundred more.

## 11 Proving up
So, would you have made it? Not just through the storm. Through all five years. Here's where the history gets honest. Around four million homestead claims were filed under the Homestead Act. The National Park Service says more than half of homesteaders proved up and got their land. Other historians count closer to forty percent. Either way, a huge number of people did what you did, paid their eighteen dollars, built their sod house, twisted their grass, and still ended up walking away. The ones who stayed got a hundred and sixty acres. Altogether, homesteaders claimed about two hundred and seventy million acres. Ten percent of the entire United States.
