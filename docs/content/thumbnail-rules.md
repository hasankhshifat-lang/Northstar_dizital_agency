# Master rules: high-CTR YouTube thumbnail creation

These rules apply to every YouTube thumbnail (long-form and Shorts) and every Facebook Page video/Reel
thumbnail. Read this file together with `.claude/skills/youtube-thumbnail-creator/SKILL.md` before making
any thumbnail.

## How these rules fit the channel's house rules

The channel rules in `CLAUDE.md` and section 0 of the thumbnail skill still hold. Where this file and those
rules differ, the channel rules win:

- **Tool:** every thumbnail is built in **Figma** (file "NorthStar Thumbnails"). Where this file says
  "generate", it means "build in Figma". The artwork is generated inside Figma with the ChatGPT image model
  (`generate_image`, model `gpt-image-2.5-flare`; owner, 2026-11-11), no text in the image, max 3 generations;
  all words are live Figma text. No image generators outside Figma, no Gemini models, no Canva, PIL, Higgsfield or
  vidIQ thumbnail generator. The owner's Figma account is on **pay-as-you-go** (owner, 2026-11-11): Figma usage or
  tool-call limits are never a reason to skip Figma; on a rate/usage limit wait ~60 s and retry (max 3 times).
- **Style:** the channel's **illustrated map style** (parchment land, teal water, tan story state, rust-red
  route, red pins, dark label tags, gold-on-dark date tag, Anton heading in white + gold) is the default.
  Apply the "cinematic" rules below (composition, hierarchy, contrast, lighting, colour discipline,
  one idea, mobile readability) to that map style. Faces and people (sections 5 and 11) are used only when
  the user asks for them.
- **Heading length:** 2–6 words (channel rule); aim for 2–5 as below.
- **Canvas:** 16:9 1280×720 for long-form/feed videos; 9:16 1080×1920 for Shorts/Reels with the bottom
  ~400 px kept clear.

## 1. Primary objective

Every thumbnail must:

- Immediately capture attention while viewers scroll.
- Create curiosity about the video's story.
- Communicate the main subject within 1–2 seconds.
- Make viewers interested enough to click.
- Create an emotional reaction.
- Stand out among competing YouTube thumbnails.
- Accurately represent the actual video.

A thumbnail is not a poster, movie cover, or summary of the entire video. It is a visual curiosity
trigger. The viewer should understand the main subject but still want to discover what happened.

## 2. Always read the full script first (mandatory)

Before creating any thumbnail:

1. Read the entire final video script.
2. Understand the complete story.
3. Identify the main character or central subject.
4. Identify the most important event.
5. Find the most surprising, mysterious, dramatic, or unexpected moment.
6. Understand the main conflict and final outcome.
7. Identify the single most interesting visual moment that can represent the story.

Never make a thumbnail based only on the video title. Never make a generic historical thumbnail without
understanding the story. The thumbnail must communicate the actual central idea of the video.

## 3. Find the strongest thumbnail angle

Before designing, identify the strongest visual selling point. Ask:

- What is the most shocking part of this story?
- What would make an ordinary American viewer curious?
- Which character or historical figure should viewers recognize?
- Is there a dramatic event that represents the story?
- Is there an interesting mystery that can be shown visually?
- Which moment would make someone ask, "What happened here?"

Example: a video about Abraham Lincoln's assassin escaping and eventually being captured.

- Weak heading: "Abraham Lincoln History"
- Better heading: "AMERICA'S MOST WANTED"
- Alternatives: "HOW DID HE ESCAPE?", "THE MANHUNT BEGINS", "THE FINAL ESCAPE"

The heading must match the actual story and the visual. Write at least three thumbnail concepts before
choosing the strongest.

## 4. One main idea per thumbnail

Never overcrowd the thumbnail. Every thumbnail has:

- One primary subject.
- One clear visual story.
- One dominant emotion or dramatic situation.
- One short headline, if text improves the concept.
- A clean, recognizable composition.

Avoid too many characters, locations, objects, or unrelated events. The viewer should understand the
thumbnail immediately, even on a small mobile screen.

## 5. Character and emotion (only when people are used)

Use expressions that fit the actual event: fear, shock, determination, anger, suspicion, desperation,
confidence, surprise.

- The main character is visually prominent.
- Facial expressions are clear and believable.
- Use appropriate eye direction and body language.
- No exaggerated cartoon expressions for serious documentaries.
- Historically appropriate clothing and appearance.
- Don't invent physical characteristics or falsely portray historical figures.

If the story is about a famous person, use a recognizable, historically grounded representation.

## 6. Composition and visual hierarchy

- Clear foreground, middle ground and background when appropriate.
- The main subject is the strongest visual element.
- Use contrast to separate the subject from the background.
- No unnecessary background detail.
- Use lighting/shading to guide attention.
- Use perspective and depth for impact.
- Leave enough negative space for the headline.
- Never crop important subjects or text.

Use the rule of thirds when it helps, but the story comes before rigid composition rules.

## 7. Colour and lighting

- Dark, dramatic tones for mystery.
- Warm historical light for emotional stories.
- High contrast for confrontations.
- Muted historical colours for documentary realism.
- A few chosen accent colours to highlight the important subject.

Strong contrast without looking artificial. Avoid: excessive saturation, too many competing colours,
unnatural skin tones, excessive glow, cheap effects, overused red arrows and circles. The palette must
match the period and mood.

## 8. Thumbnail text rules

Text strengthens the visual; it doesn't explain the whole story.

- About 2–5 powerful words; keep it short.
- Large, bold, readable type; the most important word stands out.
- Readable on mobile, with strong contrast against the background.
- No long sentences, no unnecessary subtitles.
- Check spelling and grammar.
- Never cover the main character's face (or the map's focus point).

Headline styles that work:

| Story type | Example |
|---|---|
| Mystery | "WHERE DID HE GO?" |
| Manhunt | "THE BIGGEST MANHUNT" |
| Betrayal | "BETRAYED FROM WITHIN" |
| Historical disaster | "THE NIGHT EVERYTHING CHANGED" |
| Escape | "HOW DID HE ESCAPE?" |
| Presidential history | "THE DECISION THAT CHANGED AMERICA" |

Don't give away the whole payoff unless that is the strongest selling point.

### Thumbnail and title work together

The thumbnail creates curiosity; the title adds context. Never repeat the same words in both.

## 9. Visual storytelling

The thumbnail should tell a story even without the headline. Conceptual examples (always design around
the actual video):

- A fugitive looking over his shoulder while authorities close in (on the map: an escape route with
  pursuers' pins closing in).
- A president in a dramatic historical setting.
- A mysterious empty location with one important object.
- A soldier facing an overwhelming situation.
- An investigator examining a key clue.

Use visual contrast where possible: one versus many, hunted versus hunters, light versus dark, safety
versus danger, freedom versus imprisonment, power versus defeat.

Don't add objects or events that never happened if they create a false impression of the story.

## 10. Historical authenticity (mandatory)

- Verify the historical period.
- Correct clothing, uniforms, buildings, vehicles, weapons and surroundings.
- No modern objects in historical scenes.
- Accurate flags, national symbols, state borders and place names.
- Don't combine people from different periods.
- Don't present an imagined scene as an authentic photograph.

Reconstructed scenes use cinematic historical realism, never a claim of photographic authenticity.

## 11. Thumbnail style

House style: the illustrated map style (see the top of this file). The quality bar is a premium historical
documentary campaign:

- Sharp focal subject, clean composition, high visual contrast.
- Strong visual storytelling and atmosphere.
- Professional colour grading and lighting/shading.
- Realistic textures where used; dramatic but believable expressions if people are shown.

Avoid: cheap-looking AI artwork, cartoonish historical characters, flat lighting, generic stock-photo
looks, visual clutter, unnecessary decoration.

## 12. Technical requirements

- Long-form: 1280×720, 16:9. Shorts/Reels: 1080×1920, 9:16.
- JPG or PNG, under 2 MB.
- Text and important subjects inside safe margins.
- Readable at mobile preview size.

Inspect the final export for: spelling, distorted faces, extra fingers or odd anatomy, wrong historical
details, cropped subjects, visual artifacts, poor text readability. Never deliver a thumbnail with
obvious errors.

## 13. Competitor and market research

Before finalizing:

- Study successful thumbnails in the same niche (vidIQ: outliers, similar thumbnails, competitors).
- Understand their composition and visual hierarchy, and the patterns that attract attention.
- Look for ways to be more distinctive.
- Never copy another creator's thumbnail.
- Don't reuse an identical layout for every video: keep a recognizable channel identity, but give each
  thumbnail its own concept.

## 14. Quality scoring

Score every thumbnail out of 100:

| Criteria | Points |
|---|---:|
| Immediate attention | 20 |
| Curiosity and click appeal | 20 |
| Clear storytelling | 15 |
| Visual composition | 15 |
| Headline readability | 10 |
| Emotional impact | 10 |
| Historical accuracy | 5 |
| Mobile readability | 5 |
| **Total** | **100** |

- 90–100: Excellent, ready to publish.
- 80–89: Strong, minor improvements recommended.
- 70–79: Needs improvement.
- Below 70: Redo it.

Historical accuracy and truthful representation are mandatory regardless of the score.

## 15. A/B testing

Don't assume the first thumbnail is the best. For each video, plan three genuinely different concepts:

- **Concept A, character-focused:** the main historical figure large and clear, with a strong expression
  and minimal background (only if people are used; otherwise the single key place or object, large).
- **Concept B, action-focused:** the actual dramatic event or conflict (e.g. the route, the chase, the
  disaster path on the map).
- **Concept C, mystery-focused:** an intriguing clue, location, or unexplained situation.

Use YouTube's native thumbnail testing ("Test & compare") when available. Judge the winner on
click-through rate together with watch time per impression: a thumbnail that gets clicks but disappoints
viewers is not a good thumbnail. Log the variants and the result in `social/youtube-schedule-log.md`.

## 16. Final workflow

1. Read the full script.
2. Identify the main story and the strongest visual moment.
3. Write three different thumbnail concepts (A/B/C above).
4. Write three short headline options.
5. Compare concepts on curiosity, clarity, emotion and accuracy.
6. Pick the strongest concept (keep the others as A/B test variants).
7. Build the final thumbnail in Figma.
8. Inspect the Figma screenshot/export for errors.
9. Check readability at small mobile size.
10. Score it (section 14) and redo it if needed.
11. Deliver the thumbnail with the chosen headline, the score, and a short explanation of why the concept
    fits the video.

## 17. The golden rule

- Never design a thumbnail before understanding the full story.
- Never use a generic historical image just because it looks beautiful.
- Never make the thumbnail a summary of the video.
- Make it a visual question the viewer wants answered.

The ideal viewer reaction: "What is happening here? I need to know the story."

Make them stop scrolling. Make them curious. Earn the click.
