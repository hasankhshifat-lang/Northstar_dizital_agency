# MASTER RULES: HIGH-RETENTION YOUTUBE SCRIPTWRITING & TOPIC SELECTION

Applies to every YouTube video (long-form and Shorts) and every Facebook Page video/Reel script.
Read this file before choosing a topic or writing any script.

## 1. PRIMARY OBJECTIVE

Every video must be designed to:

- Select topics that naturally attract audience curiosity.
- Capture viewers' attention within the first 3–5 seconds.
- Create curiosity that makes viewers want to discover what happens next.
- Maintain viewer interest throughout the entire video.
- Prevent unnecessary drop-offs and boring sections.
- Create emotional connections with the story.
- Encourage viewers to watch until the ending.
- Naturally encourage likes, comments, shares, and subscriptions.

Never write a script like a textbook, Wikipedia article, or ordinary historical summary.

Write like a professional documentary storyteller who understands audience psychology, suspense, and cinematic storytelling.

## 2. TOPIC SELECTION — CHOOSE STORIES PEOPLE ACTUALLY WANT TO WATCH

This is one of the most important responsibilities.

Before writing any script, research and identify topics that have strong potential to attract viewers.

Never randomly select historical events just because they are famous or easy to research.

Choose topics based on their ability to generate curiosity, emotional interest, suspense, and audience discussion.

### A. PRIORITIZE THESE TYPES OF TOPICS

1. **Unsolved Mysteries**
   - Historical disappearances.
   - Unexplained events.
   - Missing people.
   - Unanswered historical questions.
   - Mysterious deaths and identities.
   - Historical mysteries with new evidence or competing explanations.
2. **Shocking Historical Events**
   - Unexpected betrayals.
   - Failed escapes.
   - Political scandals.
   - Historical disasters.
   - Unexpected military outcomes.
   - Events that changed an entire nation.
3. **Famous People With Hidden Stories**
   - Unknown details about presidents.
   - Secret decisions made by historical leaders.
   - Unexpected personal struggles.
   - Their biggest mistakes.
   - Their rise and downfall.
   - Stories that are rarely discussed.
4. **Crime, Investigations and Escapes**
   - Famous historical criminals.
   - Manhunts.
   - Prison escapes.
   - Famous investigations.
   - Failed assassination plots.
   - How investigators solved difficult cases.
5. **Wars and Battles**
   - Impossible survival stories.
   - Unexpected victories.
   - Military deception.
   - Strategic mistakes.
   - Forgotten heroes.
   - Decisions that changed the outcome of wars.
6. **Survival and Human Courage**
   - People surviving extraordinary situations.
   - Dangerous expeditions.
   - Historical rescue operations.
   - Extraordinary acts of courage.
   - People who overcame seemingly impossible circumstances.
7. **What If and Historical Turning Points**
   - What might have happened if a major historical decision had been different?
   - Events that nearly changed American history.
   - Decisions with unexpected consequences.
   - Historical alternatives supported by credible evidence.

   Clearly label hypothetical scenarios as speculation, not historical fact.

### B. THE CURIOSITY TEST

Before approving a topic, ask:

1. Would an ordinary American viewer find this subject interesting?
2. Can the topic be explained through a compelling question?
3. Does it contain mystery, conflict, surprise, emotion, or unexpected consequences?
4. Is there an interesting development that viewers will want to discover?
5. Can this story sustain the intended video duration?
6. Is there a satisfying ending or meaningful historical revelation?
7. Can the story be presented through visually interesting scenes?

If a topic cannot generate genuine curiosity, reject it.

### C. THE TITLE POTENTIAL TEST

Before selecting a topic, imagine at least three possible YouTube titles.

Examples:

- How Did America's Most Wanted Man Finally Get Caught?
- The Decision That Nearly Destroyed an American President
- The Prison Escape That Shocked America

These are examples of title styles, not reusable titles.

If a topic cannot produce an interesting and accurate title, it may not be a strong video candidate.

Never create misleading clickbait.

### D. RESEARCH CURRENT AUDIENCE INTEREST

When selecting topics:

- Research current YouTube trends and popular historical videos.
- Examine successful videos from competing history channels.
- Look for topics receiving significant audience interest.
- Identify historical subjects gaining attention through recent documentaries, anniversaries, discoveries, or public discussions.
- Examine audience comments to discover unanswered questions.
- Look for stories other channels have covered poorly or incompletely.
- Identify opportunities to tell familiar stories from a fresh perspective.

Do not simply copy successful competitors.

Study why their topics attracted viewers and find original opportunities.

(Use the vidIQ connector for this research first, per CLAUDE.md.)

### E. PRIORITIZE EVERGREEN AND TRENDING TOPICS

Maintain a balance between:

**Evergreen topics:** stories that remain interesting regardless of the date.

- Famous historical mysteries.
- Presidential scandals.
- Major historical escapes.
- Famous criminal investigations.
- Important historical betrayals.

**Trending opportunities:** stories receiving temporary attention because of:

- Historical anniversaries.
- Newly released historical documents.
- New archaeological discoveries.
- Popular documentaries.
- Current public discussions connected to historical events.

Prioritize evergreen topics for long-term traffic while using relevant trending topics for timely opportunities.

### F. AVOID WEAK TOPICS

Reject topics that:

- Have no compelling central conflict.
- Are ordinary historical biographies without an unusual angle.
- Have insufficient reliable information.
- Require too much background before anything interesting happens.
- Have no meaningful turning point or payoff.
- Repeat stories recently published on our own channel without a genuinely new angle.
- Are interesting only to a very narrow specialist audience, unless the channel specifically targets that audience.
- Depend on exaggerated or unsupported claims.

A famous historical name does not automatically make a good video topic.

### G. TOPIC SCORING SYSTEM

Score every potential topic out of 100.

| Evaluation criteria | Points |
|---|---|
| Curiosity and mystery | 20 |
| Emotional impact | 15 |
| Suspense and storytelling potential | 15 |
| Audience interest and demand | 15 |
| Unexpected developments | 10 |
| Visual storytelling potential | 10 |
| Comment and discussion potential | 10 |
| Reliable research availability | 5 |
| **TOTAL** | **100** |

Selection rules:

- 85–100: Excellent candidate. Prioritize.
- 75–84: Strong candidate. Consider publishing.
- 65–74: Average. Look for a stronger angle.
- Below 65: Reject unless there is a special strategic reason.

A high score does not override historical accuracy or source reliability.

### H. TOPIC RESEARCH REPORT

Before writing any script, prepare a short internal topic evaluation report containing:

1. Proposed topic.
2. Why viewers may find it interesting.
3. Main historical mystery or conflict.
4. Strongest storytelling angle.
5. Potential opening hook.
6. Three possible YouTube titles.
7. Expected audience interest.
8. Evergreen or trending classification.
9. Visual storytelling opportunities.
10. Reliable research sources.
11. Topic score out of 100.

Select the strongest candidate based on the evidence, not personal preference.

If the user has already supplied a topic, evaluate its storytelling potential and suggest a stronger angle if necessary. Do not silently replace the user's requested subject.

## 3. THE FIRST 5 SECONDS — THE HOOK

The opening is the most important part of the video.

STRICT RULES:

- Never start with "Hello everyone", "Welcome back", or lengthy introductions.
- Never begin with a generic historical background.
- Never introduce the channel before introducing the story.
- Start with something surprising, mysterious, dangerous, emotional, or unexpected.
- Immediately establish a question in the viewer's mind.
- Make viewers feel that they need to know what happens next.

Use these hook techniques:

- **A. Mystery Hook:** "One man disappeared after committing a crime that changed American history. But what happened next was even more unbelievable."
- **B. Shocking Fact Hook:** "America had never seen anything like this before. And the consequences would change the nation forever."
- **C. Question Hook:** "How did one ordinary man become the most wanted person in America?"
- **D. Unexpected Twist Hook:** "Everyone believed he had escaped. But investigators were about to discover one mistake that changed everything."
- **E. Countdown Hook:** "He had only minutes to escape. Behind him, an entire nation was searching for him."

IMPORTANT: These are examples of storytelling techniques, not reusable opening templates. Create a unique hook based on the actual story.

## 4. THE OPENING 30 SECONDS

The first 30 seconds must contain:

1. A powerful opening hook.
2. A quick introduction to the central conflict.
3. An unanswered question or mystery.
4. A reason to watch until the end.

Do not reveal the entire story in the opening.

Create an information gap between what viewers know and what they want to discover.

## 5. STORY STRUCTURE

Organize every long-form script into a cinematic narrative.

**ACT 1 — THE SETUP**
- Introduce the central character, event, or historical situation.
- Establish the time and location naturally.
- Explain why the event matters.
- Introduce the central conflict.

**ACT 2 — THE ESCALATION**
- Increase tension gradually.
- Introduce unexpected developments.
- Reveal important historical details.
- Introduce obstacles, decisions, and consequences.
- Keep introducing new questions.

**ACT 3 — THE TURNING POINT**
- Reveal the most important discovery, decision, betrayal, confrontation, or unexpected event.
- Build toward the central climax.
- Make the audience understand why this moment changed everything.

**ACT 4 — THE PAYOFF**
- Answer the main question raised in the opening.
- Reveal the final outcome.
- Explain the historical consequences.
- End with a memorable final thought.

Never make the story feel like four disconnected sections. Each section must naturally lead into the next.

## 6. THE CURIOSITY LOOP SYSTEM

Create multiple curiosity loops throughout the video.

A curiosity loop means introducing an interesting unanswered question and delaying its answer until the appropriate moment.

Examples:

- "But investigators had overlooked one important detail."
- "What happened next would completely change the investigation."
- "There was just one problem with his plan."
- "However, the biggest surprise was still waiting."
- "And that is where the story takes an unexpected turn."

Rules:

- Introduce a meaningful curiosity loop every 30–45 seconds when appropriate.
- Resolve previously introduced questions.
- Introduce new questions naturally.
- Never create fake mysteries or promise twists that do not exist.
- Avoid repeating the same suspense phrases.

## 7. PATTERN INTERRUPTS

Prevent the narration from becoming predictable.

Use different storytelling approaches throughout the video:

- Unexpected historical facts.
- Short dramatic questions.
- Changes in location or time.
- Character decisions.
- Contradictions between expectations and reality.
- Important historical evidence.
- Unexpected consequences.
- Emotional moments.
- Brief changes in narration pace.

Every new section should introduce fresh information or a meaningful development.

Do not artificially insert suspense into ordinary historical details.

## 8. EMOTIONAL STORYTELLING

Make viewers feel connected to the events.

Use:

- Fear and uncertainty.
- Courage and determination.
- Betrayal and deception.
- Hope and disappointment.
- Human conflict.
- Unexpected consequences.
- Historical injustice and sacrifice.

Show the human side of history.

Instead of simply saying what happened, explain:

- What the people involved knew at that moment.
- What they were trying to achieve.
- What was at stake.
- What decisions they faced.
- How their decisions affected others.

Never invent someone's private thoughts, conversations, or emotions as historical facts. Clearly distinguish documented facts from reasonable interpretation.

## 9. PACING AND NARRATION

- Use natural conversational American English and Bangladeshi Bangla.
- Prefer clear, easy-to-understand sentences.
- Mix short dramatic sentences with longer explanatory sentences.
- Avoid unnecessarily complicated vocabulary.
- Avoid lengthy historical explanations that interrupt the story.
- Remove repetitive information.
- Every paragraph must advance the story, explain something important, or create meaningful emotional impact.
- Write for spoken narration, not academic reading.

Avoid unnecessary phrases such as:

- "Let's dive into history."
- "Now, let's go back in time."
- "As we all know."
- "Interestingly enough."

## 10. MID-VIDEO RETENTION

Never allow the middle of the video to become a boring information dump.

Every 30–60 seconds, introduce a meaningful development:

- New evidence.
- A surprising discovery.
- A major decision.
- A change in circumstances.
- A new obstacle.
- A consequence.
- A revelation.

Use transitions that make viewers curious about the next development.

Do not repeat the opening hook in different words.

## 11. THE FINAL PAYOFF

The ending must deliver what the opening promised.

Rules:

- Answer the central question.
- Resolve the main curiosity loops.
- Explain the ultimate outcome.
- Include the historical significance.
- Finish with a memorable statement.

Never abruptly end the video.

Never introduce an unrelated topic at the end.

## 12. LIKE, COMMENT AND SUBSCRIBE STRATEGY

Engagement must feel natural rather than forced.

**LIKE:** Use a like request when viewers have received meaningful value or experienced an emotional moment.
Example: "If you appreciate stories that uncover the forgotten moments of American history, give this video a like."

**COMMENT:** Ask a specific question related to the actual story. Examples:

- "Do you think the decision changed the course of American history?"
- "What would you have done in that situation?"
- "Which moment surprised you the most?"
- "Who do you think was responsible for this outcome?"

Questions must invite genuine opinions or historical discussion.

**SUBSCRIBE:** Use a short, natural subscription request near the ending.
Example: "Subscribe for more extraordinary stories hidden in American history."

IMPORTANT:

- Never interrupt the opening hook with a subscription request.
- Never repeatedly ask viewers to like and subscribe.
- Avoid generic engagement requests.
- Do not use manipulative guilt or fake urgency.
- Use one strong, relevant comment question.

## 13. HISTORICAL ACCURACY

Accuracy is more important than drama.

Before writing:

- Research the historical event.
- Verify dates, names, locations, and major events.
- Check important claims against reliable sources.
- Identify disputed historical interpretations.
- Do not invent conversations, evidence, statistics, or events.
- Do not present speculation as established fact.
- Do not sacrifice accuracy to create a dramatic twist.

Dramatic storytelling must be built around real historical events.

## 14. YOUTUBE SHORTS RULES

For Shorts (and Facebook Reels), use a different structure.

- **0–3 seconds:** Extremely powerful hook.
- **3–10 seconds:** Introduce the mystery or conflict.
- **10–35 seconds:** Develop the story with unexpected information.
- **35–50 seconds:** Deliver the major revelation or payoff.
- **Final seconds:** End with a memorable line or relevant engagement question.

Adapt the duration to the story. Do not stretch a simple story just to reach a particular length.

Avoid lengthy introductions, unnecessary explanations, and slow endings.

Every sentence must earn its place.

## 15. FINAL TOPIC AND SCRIPT QUALITY CHECK

Before submitting any video package, evaluate it using these checklists.

**TOPIC CHECK:**

- [ ] Is the topic genuinely interesting to the intended audience?
- [ ] Does it have a compelling central question?
- [ ] Is there enough storytelling potential?
- [ ] Does it have a strong and accurate title angle?
- [ ] Is there sufficient reliable research?
- [ ] Can the story support the intended video duration?
- [ ] Does it offer interesting visual opportunities?
- [ ] Has the topic been scored using the topic evaluation system?

**SCRIPT CHECK:**

- [ ] Does the first sentence immediately capture attention?
- [ ] Does the first 5 seconds create curiosity?
- [ ] Does the opening 30 seconds establish a compelling reason to continue?
- [ ] Is the central conflict clear?
- [ ] Are there meaningful curiosity loops?
- [ ] Does every section introduce useful new information?
- [ ] Is the middle engaging?
- [ ] Are there unnecessary repetitions?
- [ ] Does the story build toward a satisfying climax?
- [ ] Does the ending deliver the opening's promise?
- [ ] Is the historical information accurate?
- [ ] Is the narration natural for the intended audience?
- [ ] Is there a natural opportunity for comments?
- [ ] Is the CTA short and relevant?

If any important requirement fails, revise the topic or script before submitting it.

## 16. THE GOLDEN RULE

**NEVER SELECT A TOPIC JUST BECAUSE IT IS FAMOUS.**

**SELECT TOPICS THAT MAKE PEOPLE CURIOUS.**

**NEVER WRITE A SCRIPT JUST TO EXPLAIN WHAT HAPPENED.**

**WRITE A SCRIPT THAT MAKES VIEWERS WANT TO KNOW WHAT HAPPENS NEXT.**

Every topic, scene, sentence, transition, and revelation must serve the story.

The ultimate goal is to make viewers think: *"I need to see what happens next."*

And when the video ends: *"That was worth watching."*
